I did a broad pass across five kinds of sources: Microsoft’s own PowerPoint docs on formats, templates, sections, Zoom, and accessibility; cognitive-science guidance from Mayer/Cambridge on coherence, signaling, redundancy, and segmentation; Michael Alley’s assertion-evidence work and related research on comprehension; Nancy Duarte’s storytelling structure; and information-design guidance from NN/g, Tufte, and chart-annotation research. The big takeaway is that there is **not one single “best-looking” slide format** for complex ideas. The best decks are the ones that combine **clear story logic, one-idea-per-slide discipline, visual evidence, and controlled drill-down**. ([Microsoft Support][1])

My strongest overall recommendation is this: build modern decks as **.pptx**, not legacy **.ppt**; use **16:9 widescreen**; save your reusable system as a **.potx template**; organize long decks with **sections**; use **Summary/Section Zoom** only when drill-down navigation adds value; and share either **.pptx** for editing, **.ppsx** for show-only playback, or **PDF** for fixed distribution. Microsoft documents all of those formats and features directly, including that 16:9 is the default for new presentations and that saving as old **.ppt** turns on compatibility mode. ([Microsoft Support][1])

## The best deck formats for high-complexity ideas

### 1. The executive storyline deck

This is the best format when the audience needs to make a decision. The deck opens with the answer, then groups support underneath a single governing point, then backs each point with evidence. That is essentially what Barbara Minto’s Pyramid Principle is for: organizing ideas “as a pyramid under a single point,” and McKinsey’s own alumni material still describes it as a core framework for writing and presenting ideas. It works especially well when paired with takeaway titles, because audiences can grasp the argument by skimming the slide headlines. ([barbaraminto.com][2])

### 2. The assertion-evidence technical deck

This is the best format for science, engineering, product architecture, operations, or any idea that has to be **explained**, not merely announced. Each slide has a sentence headline that makes a claim, and the body of the slide shows the evidence: chart, diagram, process view, photo, equation, or short table. Alley’s tutorial states the core rule very clearly: build on messages, not topics, and support them with visual evidence, not bullet lists; Penn State’s publication records and related research report better audience comprehension for assertion-evidence presentations than common-practice slide styles. ([assertion-evidence.org][3])

### 3. The annotated data-story deck

This is the best format when the complexity sits inside the data. The unit of communication here is not “a slide with numbers,” but “a chart with a message.” The title states the takeaway, and annotations point the audience to the exact pattern, exception, threshold, or comparison that matters. Research on chart annotation describes annotation as essential in visual data-driven storytelling because it helps explain and emphasize core messages and specific data; Knaflic likewise argues that the title area is precious real estate and should carry the takeaway, not a bland description; and Tufte’s work centers evidence joined to narrative. 

### 4. The sparkline persuasion deck

This is the best format when the goal is buy-in for change: vision, strategy launch, fundraising, transformation, or keynote-style persuasion. Duarte’s Sparkline framework works by creating contrast between what is and what could be, so the audience feels movement rather than just receiving information. It is strongest when the slides stay extremely simple, because Duarte also advises one idea per slide and warns that crowded slides lose the audience if they cannot be understood in a few seconds. ([Duarte][4])

### 5. The layered explainer / architecture deck

This is the best format for systems thinking: platform architecture, multi-part models, end-to-end workflows, policy stacks, or anything that must be understood at several levels of abstraction. Start with the whole map, then zoom into one layer at a time. This format is especially strong in PowerPoint because Summary Zoom and Section Zoom let you move among sections and revisit pieces without breaking flow. It is also backed by multimedia-learning principles: signaling improves learning by directing attention to the relevant parts, segmenting helps when material is complex, and clear visual hierarchy guides the eye in the intended order. ([Microsoft Support][5])

### 6. The asynchronous reader deck

This is the best format when the deck will be read without you in the room. In that case, the deck has to behave more like a short report: fuller headlines, slightly denser explanatory text, stronger transitions between sections, and a more explicit storyline. NN/g specifically recommends storytelling techniques for asynchronous deliverables and emphasizes writing for the audience; that makes this format the right choice for research readouts, board pre-reads, or decision memos disguised as slides. ([Nielsen Norman Group][6])

## Which one is actually “best”?

For most high-complexity presentations, the highest-quality answer is **a hybrid**: use **executive storyline logic** at the deck level, **assertion-evidence** at the slide level, **annotated charts** for quantitative slides, and **layered drill-down** for appendices or technical depth. That combination is the cleanest because it matches how people process complex multimedia: remove the nonessential, guide attention, segment the material, and keep the visual hierarchy obvious. ([barbaraminto.com][2])

## How to build a deck like this

Start outside PowerPoint. Write the governing question, the audience, the decision or learning outcome you need, and the single point the deck must make. Then shape the argument as a pyramid: one top point, a few logically grouped supports, then evidence underneath. That is the cleanest way to prevent slide-making from becoming decoration before thinking. ([barbaraminto.com][2])

Next, write the slide titles before you design the slides. Each title should state the takeaway, not the topic. “Customer churn is concentrated in first-90-day onboarding” is a good title; “Churn analysis” is not. Duarte recommends one idea per slide, and Knaflic explicitly recommends active titles in the title bar so the audience does not miss the takeaway. ([Duarte][7])

Then design the body of each slide as evidence. Replace most bullet lists with charts, process diagrams, architecture maps, timelines, comparison tables, or labeled visuals. The cognitive-science reason is strong: the coherence principle says extraneous material hurts learning; the signaling principle says cues help direct attention; the redundancy principle warns against unnecessary duplication; and assertion-evidence research ties these ideas to better technical presentation outcomes. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][8])

Build the design system once, then reuse it. In practice that means a Slide Master with a small set of layouts: title, section divider, assertion-evidence chart, process slide, comparison slide, full-bleed image/diagram, dashboard slide, and appendix slide. Save that as a **.potx** so future decks inherit the same typography, spacing, and color logic. Microsoft also recommends accessible font format and color, and NN/g’s visual-hierarchy guidance is exactly what keeps clean decks feeling intuitive instead of merely minimal. ([Microsoft Support][9])

Finally, separate the deck into a **core path** and an **appendix**. The core path should be what any smart audience needs to understand the idea; the appendix is where proofs, methodology, assumptions, and backup data live. Use PowerPoint sections to keep this manageable, and use Zoom only when you genuinely need non-linear navigation during live delivery. ([Microsoft Support][10])

## What to avoid

The weakest format for complex ideas is still the old default: topic title plus bullet list plus decorative filler. Alley’s assertion-evidence guidance argues against topic-and-bullets; Duarte warns against crowded slides; and the coherence and redundancy principles both explain why clutter and duplicated text make understanding worse, not better. ([assertion-evidence.org][3])
