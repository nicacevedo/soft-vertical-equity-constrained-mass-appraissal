\begin{abstract}

We propose a simple regressivity-aware modification of standard regression objectives that can be incorporated into regression models commonly used in practice by assessors (e.g., linear regression \& gradient boosting). \section{Introduction}



\textit{Mass appraisal} is a critical component of local public finance: it helps local governments determine property taxes, often the largest source of local public revenue in the United States. In the \textcite{Berry2021} Census-based framing, property taxes provide 72.0\% of local tax revenue and 47\% of local own-source general revenue in the United States; roughly \$500 billion per year. The term \textit{mass appraisal} refers to the process of estimating the value of a group of real estate properties, as of a given date, for tax purposes. The process is done using common data, standardized methods, and statistical testing \cite{IAAO2025Mass}. Its importance extends beyond property owners; mainly for two reasons. First, assessments help determine property tax liabilities, which in turn finance roads, schools, and many other public services.  Second, they affect property values and housing costs more broadly, with consequences that extend to much of the population, both directly and indirectly \cite{Berry2021, Rootetal2023}. Therefore, securing an \textit{accurate} and equitable (\textit{fair}) assessment process is essential; both for local public finance and social welfare.

In recent years, local government assessors' offices have increasingly adopted Machine Learning (ML)-based methods in mass appraisal, motivated by their strong predictive performance, efficiency, and scalability in large valuation settings \cite{IAAOAITaskForce2022,BidansetRakow2022,Rootetal2023}. Commonly studied model classes in the mass appraisal literature include linear regression-based methods and tree ensembles \cite{Rootetal2023,Ostrikova2024,Sevgen2024100111}. In this setting, however, predictive gains alone are not sufficient: methods must also remain equitable, explainable, operationally feasible, and compatible with assessor workflows. Naturally, the growing use of ML raises important concerns in this matters; mainly about equity in assessment. Two of the main concerns are whether ML-based valuation can produce \textit{fair} predictions across: (i) properties with similar characteristics (\textit{horizontal equity}), and (ii) properties of different price level strata (\textit{vertical equity}) \cite{Quintos2014,10578d49-78f8-3ef9-8277-9b6add2bd071,cornia2006property}. Although both concepts have long been studied in the literature, even before the ML adoption era, vertical equity remains a persistent challenge in practice; assessments often exhibit \textit{regressivity} \cite{McMillen2020,IAAO2023VerticalReview,McMillenSingh2023}. Accordingly, this paper focuses primarily on reducing vertical equity issues, and in particular on reducing \textit{regressivity} in ML-based valuation.

\textit{Regressivity} arises when lower-valued properties tend to be over-assessed, while higher-valued properties tend to be under-assessed. This pattern has long been recognized in the mass appraisal literature and can have substantial distributional consequences for taxpayers \cite{McMillen2020}. \textcite{amornsiripanitch2020why} argues that owners of
lower-valued properties might face effective tax rates up to 50\% higher than those paid by owners of higher-valued properties. Data limitations, model misspecification, and assessment practices are often cited as contributing factors \cite{Berry2021}. The adoption of ML-based assessment workflows creates both an opportunity and a challenge in this setting. On one hand, these workflows provide a mathematical object that can be explicitly modified to mitigate regressivity (see, e.g., \textcite{CandoganHanLu2023}). On the other hand, standard objectives in regression models are designed to optimize average predictive accuracy, which tends to pull extreme predictions toward conditional averages (see Section \ref{subsec:squared_loss_regressivity}). Thus, if valuation features are incomplete or imperfectly measured in the mass appraisal context, predictive models can reinforce regressive assessment patterns \cite{amornsiripanitch2020why, CandoganHanLu2023}.

To evaluate assessment equity, we follow the standard framework used in ratio studies by the International Association of Assessing Officers (IAAO) \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}. Many of these measures are based on the \textit{assessment-to-sale ratio}, defined as $r := \hat{P}/P$, where $\hat{P}$ denotes the assessed value and $P$ the market value of a property. Under regressive assessment, these ratios tend to decrease with property value: lower-tier properties are more likely to have ratios above one, while higher-tier properties are more likely to have ratios below one. Figure~\ref{fig:regressivity_motivation} illustrates this phenomenon. Motivated by this negative pattern, we study an in-processing approach that penalizes systematic dependence between assessment ratios and property values, with the goal of reducing regressivity while preserving similar predictive performance levels.

\paragraph{Contributions.} Our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We develop a simple, in-processing approach for mitigating regressivity in ML-based mass appraisal by augmenting standard regression losses with soft covariance-based regularization terms.\footnote{Related dependence-regularization ideas appear in the fair-ML literature; see, e.g., \cite{lee2022maximal,LiEtAl2022Fairness,ScutariPaneroProissl2022}.}

    \item We show how this approach can be incorporated into regression models commonly used in practice by assessors; including linear regression and gradient boosting. This provides a practical way to reduce vertical inequity without requiring a complete redesign of existing valuation workflows.

    \item Using the real-world Cook County Assessor's Office mass appraisal pipeline, we show empirically that the proposed corrections can improve equity--accuracy tradeoffs under standard assessment metrics, and in some settings improve both simultaneously.

    \item We formalize a multi-objective model-selection framework for calibration that targets Pareto-efficient tradeoffs between predictive accuracy and equity measures. In particular, the framework can recover solutions that prioritize predictive performance subject to equity requirements, and it also admits convex staking extensions.\footnote{For related Pareto/fairness-frontier formulations and model-stacking ideas, see, e.g., \cite{martinez2020minimax,little2022fairness}.}
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper reviews the relevant background on mass-appraisal equity and fairness-aware regression, develops the proposed regularization and model-selection framework, and evaluates the resulting methods on a real-world assessment pipeline. 

\section{Literature Review}

In this section, we try to bridge two bodies of work that have largely developed in parallel: the ML-based mass-appraisal literature, which has mainly emphasized predictive performance model comparison in property valuation \cite{Rootetal2023,Zilli2024}, and the fairness-in-ML literature, which has developed general frameworks for fair regression, fairness regularization, and fairness--accuracy tradeoff analysis in predictive models \cite{CatonHaas2024,AgarwalDudikWu2019,FitzsimonsEtAl2019,lee2022maximal,LiEtAl2022Fairness}. 


\subsection{ML in Mass Appraisal and Assessor Workflows}

Recent reviews show that mass appraisal and real-estate valuation research have moved steadily machine learning (ML) methods, with multiple regression, tree ensembles, and neural-network-based models among the most frequently studied approaches \cite{WangLi2019,Rootetal2023,Zilli2024,purdueAssessingTechniques}. Across this literature, model evaluation is still dominated by predictive criteria (typically accuracy and model comparison across algorithms) rather than by equity as an explicit model-design target \cite{WangLi2019,Rootetal2023,Zilli2024}. In particular, recent reviews identify Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and related ensemble methods as some of the most commonly deployed and best-performing in practice \cite{Rootetal2023,Sevgen2024100111,Zilli2024}; any practical equity intervention should therefore be compatible with these learners.

At the same time, the adoption of ML in assessment offices remains partial and uneven. \textcite{BidansetRakow2022} report that perceived barriers include communication challenges, technical capacity, and budget constraints. The \textcite{IAAOAITaskForce2022} emphasizes explainability, defensibility, administrative feasibility, and integration into office workflows as central conditions for real-world adoption. Our focus is therefore not only on which models predict most accurately, but also on how equity-aware modifications can be incorporated into assessor practice in a way that remains operationally feasible and publicly defensible \cite{IAAOAITaskForce2022,BidansetRakow2022}. These practical constraints motivate an approach that can be layered onto existing regression objectives and familiar model classes, rather than requiring entirely new architectures or training pipelines.






\subsection{Equity in Mass Appraisal: Horizontal Equity, Vertical Equity, and Regressivity}


Although the ML literature has emphasized predictive performance, equity has long been a core objective in mass appraisal practice and standards \cite{IAAO2013Ratio,IAAO2025Mass,Quintos2014}. In assessor practice, this objective is commonly decomposed into \emph{horizontal equity}, which concerns the uniform treatment of similarly situated properties, and \emph{vertical equity}, which concerns the absence of systematic price-related bias across the value distribution \cite{10578d49-78f8-3ef9-8277-9b6add2bd071,cornia2006property,McMillenSingh2023}. The basic diagnostic framework remains the sales-ratio studies, stratified evaluation across relevant sub-markets or property classes, and careful sales verification as the foundation for evaluating mass-appraisal performance \cite{IAAO2013Ratio,IAAO2020SalesVerification,IAAO2025Mass}. Within this framework, the coefficient of dispersion (COD) serves as a standard measure of uniformity, while the price-related differential (PRD) and price-related bias (PRB) remain central diagnostics for vertical equity \cite{IAAO2013Ratio,IAAO2025Mass}.


More recently, the assessor literature has become more cautious about relying on any single measure of vertical equity. \textcite{IAAO2023VerticalReview} compare the behavior of several vertical-equity diagnostics and recommend reporting a \emph{suite} of tests rather than privileging one statistic. \textcite{McMillenSingh2023} further show that some regression-based procedures for detecting regressivity can be severely biased when assessments themselves are generated from regression-based valuation models, and they argue for complementing classical statistics such as the PRD with broader distribution-based diagnostics.




Beyond diagnostics, recent policy-oriented work shows that regressivity is not an isolated local anomaly but a widespread and substantively important feature of property taxation \cite{Berry2021,AvenancioLeonHoward2022}. Using millions of residential sales, \textcite{Berry2021} finds that assessments are typically regressive, that this pattern is widespread across U.S.\ counties, and that it is more consistent with limitations in assessors' data and models than with simple measurement error or explicit policy choices alone. Assessor-oriented studies also emphasize that institutional design and empirical design matter: \textcite{ScottDaigle2022} document a program that reduced both vertical and horizontal inequity, while \textcite{Rakow2023, Rakow2024} shows that measured local regressivity and its effective incidence can depend on assessment timing, exemptions, and policy design.

Finally, a smaller but especially relevant strand of the assessor literature moves from equity measurement to equity correction within the valuation pipeline itself. These approaches include a two-step spatial-lag/spatial-quantile procedure \cite{Quintos2014}, geographically weighted regression \cite{BidansetEtAl2019, BidansetEtAl2025}, and incorporating ratio studies into model validation steps \cite{HermansEtAl2022, HermansEtAl2025}. Further, \textcite{CandoganHanLu2023} explicitly study fairness-aware valuation models. 

Taken together, this literature shows that equity in mass appraisal does not arise automatically from better prediction alone; rather, it increasingly requires explicit measurement, explicit monitoring, and, in some settings, explicit correction within the modeling and calibration process. For the present paper, the key implication is that vertical inequity is already understood by assessors as a systematic dependence pattern between assessment outcomes and property value level, which makes it a natural target for methods that explicitly control such relationships during training.

\subsection{Fairness in Machine Learning}

Most of the fairness-in-ML literature was originally developed for classification problems, but many real applications involve continuous prediction rather than binary decisions \cite{CatonHaas2024,BarocasHardtNarayanan2023}. In those settings, fairness cannot usually be reduced to a single thresholded decision without discarding information about score magnitudes, residual structure, and calibration. For that reason, a substantial literature has developed around \emph{fair regression}: methods that treat fairness as an explicit objective when the prediction target is continuous \cite{AgarwalDudikWu2019,FitzsimonsEtAl2019,ChzhenEtAl2020Wasserstein,ChzhenEtAl2020Recalibration,WeiEtAl2023}.

A recurring conclusion of this literature is that no single fairness notion is universally preferred for regression. Depending on the application, one may want parity in the marginal distribution of predictions, parity of losses or residuals across groups, equality of conditional means, or some broader structural notion of non-discrimination \cite{AgarwalDudikWu2019,CatonHaas2024,BarocasHardtNarayanan2023}. Methodologically, the state of the art has moved from ad hoc heuristics toward constrained or regularized empirical risk minimization. \textcite{AgarwalDudikWu2019} formulate fair regression under statistical-parity and bounded-group-loss constraints and provide reduction-based algorithms with guarantees for broad classes of losses. \textcite{FitzsimonsEtAl2019} introduce a general framework based on group fairness in expectation that can be integrated into a variety of regression learners. \textcite{WeiEtAl2023} develop an RKHS formulation for mean-parity fair regression with multiple sensitive attributes. A complementary line studies distributional fairness through optimal transport and post-processing: \textcite{ChzhenEtAl2020Wasserstein} characterize demographic-parity regression via Wasserstein barycenters, while \textcite{ChzhenEtAl2020Recalibration} derive a plug-in-and-recalibration approach with statistical guarantees. Regression-specific optimization work also includes explicit dependence constraints: \textcite{KomiyamaEtAl2018} measure unfairness through correlation or coefficient-of-determination constraints, and \textcite{ScutariPaneroProissl2022} show that fairness can be controlled through a simple ridge-type penalization strategy. Taken together, this literature establishes a useful perspective for the present paper: in continuous prediction problems, fairness can be built directly into the training or calibration process rather than being treated only as an ex-post diagnostic.

\subsubsection{Dependence-Based Fairness Regularization and Fair Regression with Continuous Attributes}

A relevant idea for this work is that unfairness can often be represented as some undesired statistical relationship between model outputs and some variable of quantity of concern. Fairness corrections explicitly penalize that relationship during training. This dependence perspective is specially relevant when the source of unfairness are continuous quantities.

Several lines of work develop this idea in different ways. \textcite{MaryEtAl2019} extend fairness-aware learning to continuous sensitive attributes and treatments using the R\'enyi maximal correlation coefficient as a differentiable dependence measure. \textcite{KomiyamaEtAl2018} formulate regression fairness directly through correlation and coefficient-of-determination constraints. \textcite{lee2022maximal} further develop maximal-correlation-based regularization as a general framework for imposing fairness criteria through dependence control. In parallel, \textcite{PerezSuayEtAl2017} and \textcite{LiEtAl2022Fairness} use kernel-based dependence measures, especially HSIC and related cross-covariance operators, to build tractable dependence-regularized learners, and \textcite{BuylDefranceDeBie2024FAIRRET} provide a recent modular framework for differentiable fairness regularization terms.

These dependence-based regularizers are often attractive in practice since they can be added into standard optimization pipelines with (relatively) minimal changes to the underlying learner \cite{KomiyamaEtAl2018,MaryEtAl2019,LiEtAl2022Fairness,lee2022maximal,BuylDefranceDeBie2024FAIRRET}. Further, they are natural choices in settings where the relevant source of inequity is a continuous or an ordered variable rather than a classical protected demographic attribute. This makes dependence-based fair-regression methods methodologically closer to the present problem: a dependence between assessment ratios and sale prices. Thus, fair-regression theory offers assessors a practical toolkit for turning an observed inequity pattern into an explicit modeling target.

\subsubsection{Fairness--Accuracy Tradeoffs and Multi-Objective Model Selection}

Different models, penalties, and post-processing choices trace out different combinations of predictive performance and fairness, so model selection in out setting should be understood as choosing among tradeoffs rather than identifying a single ``best model'' \cite{CatonHaas2024}. \textcite{martinez2020minimax} formulate fairness explicitly as a multi-objective optimization problem and characterize Pareto-efficient solutions that avoid unnecessary harm across groups. \textcite{little2022fairness} study the empirical fairness--accuracy Pareto frontier directly, proposing tools to quantify and optimize model tradeoffs through frontier-based summaries rather than one-number comparisons.

This perspective is natural for the present application. In assessor workflows, model calibration already requires comparing many candidate specifications across multiple out-of-sample criteria, such as accuracy, dispersion, and regressivity diagnostics. Hence, fairness-aware mass appraisal does not create a wholly new decision problem; it extends an existing calibration problem by adding equity as an additional objective.

\subsection{Positioning of the Present Work}

 
The present paper lies at the intersection of two main bodies of work. On one hand, the mass-appraisal literature has developed standards, diagnostics, and application-specific corrections for horizontal and vertical equity, treats them primarily as a ratio-study outcome, or post hoc procedures \cite{Quintos2014,IAAO2023VerticalReview,BidansetEtAl2019,CandoganHanLu2023}. On the other hand, the fairness in machine learning literature has developed general constrained and regularized approaches for continuous prediction, including dependence-based penalties and fairness--accuracy frontier methods, but typically in settings defined by demographic protected attributes rather than assessor-style notions of vertical inequity \cite{AgarwalDudikWu2019,LiEtAl2022Fairness,lee2022maximal,martinez2020minimax,little2022fairness}. Our goal is to connect these two literatures in an assessor-compatible way to answer: (i) how to construct a regressivity-aware learner, and also (ii) how to calibrate and select candidate models when accuracy and equity move in different directions.

\subsection{Assessment Ratios and Equity Measures}
\label{subsec:equity_metrics}

For any fitted model and any evaluation sample $S$, let $\widehat P_i$ denote the fitted predicted price value of property $i$, and $P_i$ its respective sale price, and define the \emph{assessment-to-sale ratio}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ratio_def}
r_i := \frac{\widehat P_i}{P_i},
\qquad \forall i\in S,
\end{equation}
where dependence on the fitted model is suppressed when clear from context. We also refer to this object simply as the \textit{assessment ratio}.

\textit{Ratio studies} are the set of metrics defined around the assessment-to-sale ratios, to measure the validity of an assessment \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}. We focus on defining the equity diagnostics defined by the IAAO, and also the central statistics around the ratios, which are a complementary measure of equity and assessment behavior, referred to as the \textit{assessment level}. 

\paragraph{Remark.} All equity diagnostics in this paper are computed on the price scale of $\widehat P_i$ and $P_i$, even though the underlying predictive models specified in later sections are trained on the log-prices scale.

\paragraph{Assessment level.}
To measure the \textit{assessment level} \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}, assessors examine central statistics of the ratio sample $\{r_i\}_{i\in S}$, including the median, mean, weighted mean, and standard deviation. Let
\[
m_S := \operatorname{med}\{r_i: i\in S\}
\]
denote the median ratio,
\[
\bar r (S) := \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S} r_i
\]
the mean ratio,
\[
\overline{r_W} (S) := \frac{1}{\sum_{i\in S} P_i}\sum_{i\in S} r_i P_i
\]
the value-weighted mean ratio, and
\[
\sigma_S := \sqrt{\frac{1}{|S|-1}\sum_{i\in S}(r_i-\bar r)^2}
\]
the sample standard deviation of the assessment ratios. We omit the dependence on $S$ when the context is clear.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Horizontal equity measures.}
We summarize \textit{horizontal equity} primarily through the coefficient of dispersion (COD), which the IAAO describes as the average percentage deviation from the median ratio $m_S$ \cite{IAAO2013Ratio,IAAO2025ExposureRatio}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cod}
\operatorname{COD}(S)
:=
100\cdot\frac{1}{|S|}
\sum_{i\in S}\frac{|r_i-m_S|}{m_S}.
\end{equation}
Lower COD values indicate greater within-group uniformity. We also report the coefficient of variation (COV), defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cov_metric}
\operatorname{COV}(S)
:=
100\cdot\frac{\sigma_S}{\bar r},
\end{equation}
as a supplementary dispersion diagnostic. Both indicators summarize how dispersed the ratios are around a central tendency.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Vertical equity measures.}
We summarize vertical equity using standard \textit{price-related diagnostics}. First, the price-related differential (PRD) is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prd}
\operatorname{PRD}(S)
:=
\frac{\bar r}{\overline{r_W}}.
\end{equation}
Under standard assessor interpretation, PRD values above $1.03$ tend to indicate regressivity, while values below $0.98$ tend to indicate progressivity \cite{IAAO2013Ratio,IAAO2025ExposureRatio}.

We also report the coefficient of price-related bias (PRB), defined in terms of a quantity referred to as the \textit{market-value proxy}; \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}
$
\operatorname{MV}_i
:=
\log_2\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(P_i+\frac{\widehat P_i}{m_S}\right)\right),
$
designed to reduce measurement-error bias \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}. The PRB is then defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prb}
\operatorname{PRB}(S)
:=
\operatorname{slope}\!\left(\frac{r_i-m_S}{m_S}\sim \operatorname{MV}_i\right).
\end{equation}
That is, PRB is the slope of relative ratio deviations from the median against the market-value proxy. PRB values below $-0.05$ suggest regressivity, while values above $0.05$ suggest progressivity \cite{IAAO2013Ratio,IAAO2025ExposureRatio}.

Finally, we report the vertical equity indicator (VEI), recently introduced in the IAAO 2025 exposure draft \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}, defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:vei}
\operatorname{VEI}(S)
:=
100\cdot\frac{m_{S_{q_{10}}}-m_{S_{q_1}}}{m_S},
\end{equation}
where $m_{S_{q_{10}}}$ is the median ratio in the highest value decile of $S$ (ranked by the proxy $\operatorname{MV}_i$) and $m_{S_{q_1}}$ is the median ratio in the lowest value decile. VEI values below $-10\%$ suggest regressivity, while values above $10\%$ suggest progressivity \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}.

\subsection{Regressivity as a Dependence Problem}
\label{subsec:dependence_problem}

The assessor literature usually describes regressivity as a \emph{price-related} pattern in the ratios: as property value increases, assessment ratios tend to fall. Figure \ref{fig:regressivity_motivation_boxed} illustrates this behavior through the negative trend between the ratios $\{r_i\}_{i\in S}$ and the log-prices $\{y_i\}_{i\in S}$, where $y_i=\log P_i$. We highlight the under-assessment region and the over-assessment region to make this negative pattern explicit. Log-scale is used in the figure since property values are typically highly skewed, so plotting against log-price makes the price-related trend easier to visually interpret across different sale price levels.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Fairness Price/GE Paper/img/motivation/Baseline LightGBM_0_motivation_squared_only.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of regressive behavior in mass appraisal. Assessment-to-sale ratios $r$ tend to decrease with property value $P$: lower-value properties are more likely to be over-assessed ($r > 1$), while higher-value properties are more likely to be under-assessed ($r < 1$).}
    \label{fig:regressivity_motivation_boxed}
\end{figure}

Since both ratios and prices are continuous quantities, a natural way to summarize their linear dependence is the empirical covariance:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sample_cov}
\operatorname{Cov}(r,P)
:=
\frac{1}{|S|}
\sum_{i\in S}
(r_i-\bar r)(P_i-\bar P),
\end{equation}
where $\bar r$ and $\bar P$ denote the sample means of $\{r_i\}_{i\in S}$ and $\{P_i\}_{i\in S}$, respectively. Furthermore, if $\Var(r)>0$ and $\Var(P)>0$, the sign of the covariance also determines the sign of the correlation and of the simple-regression slope of $r$ on $P$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cov_corr_slope_sign}
\operatorname{sign}\!\left(\Cov(r,P)\right)
=
\operatorname{sign}\!\left(\Corr(r,P)\right)
=
\operatorname{sign}\!\left(\operatorname{slope}(r \sim P)\right),
\end{equation}
where
$
\Corr(r,P):=\frac{\Cov(r,P)}{\sqrt{\Var(r)\Var(P)}}$, $
\operatorname{slope}(r \sim P):=\frac{\Cov(r,P)}{\Var(P)}$, and $\operatorname{sign}(\cdot) \in \{-1,0,1\}$ is the signum function.
Thus, $\Cov(r,P)<0$ precisely indicates a (linear) price-related negative pattern, which we associate with regressivity.

Moreover, the covariance is directionally aligned with classical assessor-side regressivity diagnostics. In particular, it has the same directional ordering as the price-related differential (PRD) \cite{IAAO2025ExposureRatio}. This is formalized in Proposition \ref{prop:cov_prd}.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:cov_prd}
Let $P>0$ and $r=\widehat P/P>0$ almost surely, with $\Expected{r}<\infty$, $\Expected{P}<\infty$, and $\Expected{rP}<\infty$. Then the population covariance  and the population
$
\mathrm{PRD}=\frac{\Expected{r}\Expected{P}}{\Expected{rP}}
$
satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cov_prd_sign}
\operatorname{sign}\!\left(\Cov(r,P)\right)
=
\operatorname{sign}\!\left(1-\mathrm{PRD}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since
$
\Cov(r,P)=\Expected{rP}-\Expected{r}\Expected{P},
$
we have
\[
1-\mathrm{PRD}
=
1-\frac{\Expected{r}\Expected{P}}{\Expected{rP}}
=
\frac{\Expected{rP}-\Expected{r}\Expected{P}}{\Expected{rP}}
=
\frac{\Cov(r,P)}{\Expected{rP}}.
\]
Because $r>0$ and $P>0$ almost surely, $\Expected{rP}>0$. Therefore, $1-\mathrm{PRD}$ and $\Cov(r,P)$ have the same sign.
\end{proof}

Proposition \ref{prop:cov_prd} shows that covariance is directionally consistent with a standard price-related diagnostic used in assessment practice. This makes it a useful bridge between classical ratio-study interpretation and the dependence-based formulations developed in later sections.

\subsection{Mass-Appraisal Prediction Setting}
\label{subsec:prediction_setting}

% Mass appraisal estimates the value of a \emph{group} of properties as of a given date using common data, standardized methods, and statistical testing \cite{IAAO2025Mass}. 

In the predictive setting studied here, we observe a sample of $n$ sold properties $
\mathcal D_n := \{(x_i,P_i,t_i)\}_{i=1}^n,
$
where $x_i\in\mathcal X\subseteq \R^p$ is the vector of $p$ observed characteristics for property $i$ (e.g., squared footage, number of
rooms, distance to a bus stop, etc.), $P_i\in\mathbb R_{++}$ is its verified sale price, and $t_i$ is its sale date. Because prices are positive and strongly right-skewed, we work on the log-price scale and define
$
y_i := \log P_i,
\, \forall i\in[n].
$

Let $\Theta$ denote a parameter space of some predictor class, and let
$
f_\theta:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R,$
with $
\theta\in\Theta ,
$
denote a candidate predictor of log-price (e.g., $f_\theta(x_i)=\theta^\top x_i$ in linear models). For each property $i\in[n]$, the predictor produces the log-scale prediction
$
\hat y_i := f_\theta(x_i),
$
and the corresponding prediction on the original price scale is
$
\widehat P_i := \exp(\hat y_i).
$

% \paragraph{Remark.} Assessor-facing diagnostics in Section \ref{subsec:equity_metrics} are computed on the original sale-price scale; using $P_i$ and $\widehat P_i=\exp(\hat y_i)$. This is deliberate: the workflow is evaluated through the price-scale assessment ratios, not through recovery of a bias-corrected conditional mean on the original price scale. Therefore, models are trained on the log-price scale and evaluated on the price scale.

\paragraph{Log-space residuals as ratio-proxy.} The predictive models are trained on the log-price scale, and thus we define the \textit{log-scale residuals} as
$
e_i := \hat y_i-y_i, \, \forall i \in [n]
$, which sign fits better our setting.
This is exactly the logarithm of the assessment ratios:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:log_ratio_identity}
e_i
=
\hat y_i-y_i
=
\log \widehat P_i-\log P_i
=
\log\!\left(\frac{\widehat P_i}{P_i}\right)
=
\log r_i,
\qquad \forall i\in S.
\end{equation}
Hence, we can characterize regressivity in the log-scale with the log-residuals, given
\[
\operatorname{sign}(e_i)=\operatorname{sign}(r_i-1),
\qquad \forall i\in S.
\]
That is, the sign of $e$ retains the interpretation of over- and under-assessment. This identity gives us a direct log-scale counterpart to the price-related dependence discussed cussed in Section \ref{subsec:dependence_problem}, to work with; the log-scale covariance $\Cov(e, y) = \Cov(\log r, \log P)$.

\subsubsection{Why regressive patterns are expected in squared-loss learning}
\label{subsec:squared_loss_regressivity}

We briefly characterize one of the expected behaviors of regression models, and how this property connects with our dependence-based understanding of regressivity discussed in Section \ref{subsec:dependence_problem}. In the squared-loss setting, $\ell(f(x_i), y_i)=(f(x_i)- y_i)^2$, the baseline predictor $f_{\mathrm{base}}$ targets the conditional mean of log-price given the observed features; $\Expected{y|x}$. When conditional log-price variation remains unexplained, this induces a systematic non-positive linear dependence between log-residuals and log-prices. We formalize this in Proposition \ref{prop:population_covariance_identity}.


% We next explain why squared-loss learning can create regressive pressure even before any explicit fairness criterion is imposed. Because the squared-loss Bayes predictor targets conditional mean behavior, unresolved variation around that conditional mean can translate into systematic dependence between residuals and value level. The following proposition makes this precise.

% We further motivate the necessity of regressivity correction in general learning problems, such as $\eqref{opt:base_model}$; now from a theoretical perspective. Error-based learning problems naturally induce regressive patterns when some conditional price variation remains unexplained given the available features (as expected in practice). Intuitively, machine learning models aim to learn central tendency behaviors, hence pushing extreme values to some central measure of tendency. This means that extreme lower values are pushed towards the over-prediction, and the opposite for extreme higher values. We formalize this intuition for one of the most common risk measure in ML-based mass appraisal; the squared-error loss.  


\begin{proposition}[Population covariance non-positivity under squared-error prediction]
\label{prop:population_covariance_identity}

Let $Y:=\log P$ be the log-sale price, let $X$ denote the observed feature vector, and assume $\E[Y^2]<\infty$ and $\E[P^2]<\infty$. Let
$
f^\star(X)=\E[Y\mid X]
$
be the Bayes predictor under squared-error loss. Define the associated population residual
$
e^\star:=f^\star(X)-Y.
$

Let $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be a measurable nondecreasing function such that $\E[g(Y)^2]<\infty$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:covariance_population_identity}
\Cov(e^\star,g(Y))
=
-\E\!\big[\Cov(Y,g(Y)\mid X)\big]
\le 0.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the law of total covariance,
\[
\Cov(e^\star,g(Y))
=
\E\!\big[\Cov(e^\star,g(Y)\mid X)\big]
+
\Cov\!\big(\E[e^\star\mid X],\,\E[g(Y)\mid X]\big).
\]
Since
\[
\E[e^\star\mid X]=\E[\E[Y\mid X]-Y\mid X]=0,
\]
the second term is zero. Moreover, conditional on $X$, the quantity $\E[Y\mid X]$ is constant, so
\[
\Cov(e^\star,g(Y)\mid X)
=
\Cov(\E[Y\mid X]-Y,\;g(Y)\mid X)
=
-\Cov(Y,g(Y)\mid X).
\]
Hence
\[
\Cov(e^\star,g(Y))
=
-\E\!\big[\Cov(Y,g(Y)\mid X)\big].
\]
Finally, since $g$ is nondecreasing, Chebyshev's covariance inequality applied under the conditional law of $Y\mid X$ yields
\[
\Cov(Y,g(Y)\mid X)\ge 0
\qquad \text{a.s.}
\]
Therefore,
\[
\Cov(e^\star,g(Y))\le 0.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{General Constrained and Penalized Setting}
\label{subsec:general_penalized_setting}

Previous sections identify graphically and from a population perspective that ML-based mass appraisal can induce value-dependent distortions. Motivated by those observations, we now introduce an abstract correction class that constraints or penalizes sample summaries of these dependencies.

\paragraph{A (soft) constrained learning perspective.} Let $\Psi(f, \D_n)$ denote a sample-dependent functional that quantifies value-dependent distortions in the fitted log-residuals, or equivalently in the fitted log-assessment ratios. One may reformulate the baseline problem \eqref{opt:base_model} as an exact constrained learning problem of the form
\begin{align}
\label{opt:general_constrained_model}
\min_{f\in\H}
\quad
& L(f, \D_n)+\Omega(f)
\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \Psi(f, \D_n)\le \tau,
\nonumber
\end{align}
for some dependence tolerance level $\tau\ge 0$. This constrained-form idea is useful conceptually because it makes the dependence tolerance explicit; one can directly choose the maximum level of regressive dependency $\Psi(f, \D_n)$ allowed in the learning process. 


In practice, however, we work with its soft-constrained penalized counterpart, which is more convenient for estimation and hyperparameter tuning across different model classes. That is, we define the \textit{penalized learning problem}
\begin{align}
\label{opt:general_penalized_model}
f_\rho
\in
\arg\min_{f\in\H}
\left\{
L(f, \D_n)+\Omega(f)+\rho\,\Psi(f, \D_n)
\right\},
\end{align}
where $\rho\ge 0$ controls the trade-off between fitting performance and regressivity correction. Note the baseline model \eqref{opt:base_model} is recovered as the special case when $\rho=0$.

Formulation \eqref{opt:general_penalized_model} isolates the general structure of the proposed approach: starting from a standard predictive learner, we augment the empirical objective with a term that discourages systematic value-dependent distortions in the resulting assessments. The next sections make this idea concrete through covariance-based choices of $\Psi$, and then shows how those choices can be implemented in some specific model classes; linear models and boosted trees.


\section{Covariance-Based Penalization in Standard Learners}
\label{sec:covariance_penalty_learning_in_learners}

\subsection{Covariance-Based Penalization}
\label{sec:covariance_penalty_learning}

We now instantiate the general penalized learning problem \eqref{opt:general_penalized_model} by specifying concrete choices of the functional $\Psi$ based on empirical covariance-based measures. By \eqref{eq:log_ratio_identity}, we have the log-residual-ratio identity $e_i(f)=\log r_i(f)$. Thus, penalizing dependence between $e(f):=(e_1(f),\dots,e_n(f))$ and $y:=(y_1,\dots,y_n)$ is equivalent to penalizing dependence between fitted log-assessment ratios and log-sale prices.

% Since regressivity corresponds to a systematic decrease of assessment ratios with property values, covariance-based penalties provide a natural way to instantiate the abstract correction class introduced in the previous section.

% \subsubsection{Empirical covariance target}


\subsubsection{Squared covariance penalty}

Recall from Section \ref{subsec:dependence_problem} that the empirical covariance $\widehat \Cov(e(f),y)$ is directly connected to the slope of the least-squares regression of log-residuals on log-prices $y_i$, through
$
\operatorname{slope}{(e(f)\sim y)}
=
\frac{\widehat{\Cov}(e(f),y)}{\widehat{\Var}(y)},
$
and note that $\widehat{\Var}(y)$ does not depend on the model choice. Accordingly, controlling the empirical covariance is a natural way to control first-order value-dependent trends in the fitted assessment ratios.


Hence, a first class of penalty is obtained by penalizing directly the magnitude of the empirical covariance directly. The most symmetric choice is to penalize the squared covariance:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:cov2_penalty}
    \Psi^{\mathrm{cov}^2}(f)
    :=
    \frac{1}{2}\,
    \widehat{\Cov}(e(f),y)^2.
\end{align}
Substituting \eqref{eq:cov2_penalty} into \eqref{opt:general_penalized_model} yields
\begin{align}
    \label{opt:cov2_penalized_model}
    f_{\rho}^{\mathrm{cov}^2}
    \in
    \arg\min_{f\in\H}
    \left\{
        L(f)+\Omega(f)
        +
        \frac{\rho}{2}\,\widehat{\Cov}(e(f),y)^2
    \right\}.
\end{align}
This is the most direct dependence-control idea developed from the motivation in the previous sections. It suppresses both regressive and progressive linear trends, and is therefore appropriate when the goal is to drive the first-order dependence toward zero. 

\subsubsection{A separable surrogate penalty}

While the direct covariance penalty above is conceptually natural, it has two important limitations. First, covariance is a first-order summary and may fail to reflect more complex dependence patterns when positive and negative local trends cancel each other out. Second, the term $\widehat{\Cov}(e(f),y)^2$ is non-separable across observations, which can be inconvenient in optimization methods that rely on observation-wise gradients or second-order approximations.

To obtain a computationally simpler covariance-like regularizer, we upper-bound the squared empirical covariance as
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:covariance_upper_bound}
    \widehat{\Cov}_n(e(f),y)^2
    &=
    \left(
        \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n
        \bigl(e_i(f)-\bar e(f)\bigr)\bigl(y_i-\bar y\bigr)
    \right)^2
    \le
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n
    \bigl(e_i(f)-\bar e(f)\bigr)^2
    \bigl(y_i-\bar y\bigr)^2,
\end{align}
where the inequality follows from Jensen's inequality. This upper bound is still coupled across through the sample mean $\bar e(f)$. To obtain a separable term, we drop the centering of the residuals and consider the simplified surrogate
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:surrogate_penalty}
    \Psi^{\mathrm{surr}}(f)
    :=
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n
    e_i(f)^2\bigl(y_i-\bar y\bigr)^2.
\end{align}
Substituting \eqref{eq:surrogate_penalty} into \eqref{opt:general_penalized_model} gives the surrogate-penalized problem
\begin{align}
    \label{opt:surrogate_penalized_model}
    f_{\rho}^{\mathrm{surr}}
    \in
    \arg\min_{f\in\H}
    \left\{
        L(f)+\Omega(f)
        +
        \rho\,\Psi^{\mathrm{surr}}(f)
    \right\}.
\end{align}
Thus, $\Psi^{\mathrm{surr}}$ should be viewed as a separable approximation motivated by covariance control, rather than as exact control of $\widehat{\Cov}_n(e(f),y)^2$ itself.

\paragraph{Remark.} Note that we suppressing the average log-residuals $\Bar e(f)$, we are penalizing the distance between $e_i(f)$ and 0 instead of their distances to the average log-residual. If working on the original price scale, the analogous penalization would be the distance $(r_i - 1)^2$.  


% The surrogate \eqref{eq:surrogate_penalty} has two useful features. First, it remains anchored to the same underlying idea as the covariance-based penalties: residual distortions are weighted more heavily when they occur far from the center of the value distribution. Second, unlike \eqref{eq:cov2_penalty}, it is separable across observations once the weights $(y_i-\bar y)^2$ are fixed. This makes \eqref{opt:surrogate_penalized_model} particularly attractive for model classes and optimization procedures in which observation-wise objective contributions are computationally convenient.

% \subsubsection{Penalty comparison and methodological roles}

\subsection{Model-Specific Implementations}
\label{sec:model_specific_implementations}

We now show how the covariance-based penalization terms introduced in Section~\ref{sec:covariance_penalty_learning} can be instantiated in specific model classes. The goal is to show that the previously defined covariance-based corrections can be embedded into predictive tools already being used in mass-appraisal workflows. We focus on two especially relevant examples: linear models and second-order tree boosting. The former provides a transparent analytical benchmark that clarifies the algebraic effect of each penalty, while the latter covers widely used high-performing nonlinear learners such as XGBoost- and LightGBM-type methods. 

% These implemented learners will later define the candidate model configurations compared in the constrained calibration procedure of Section~\ref{sec:model_selection}.

\subsubsection{Linear Covariance-Penalized Models}
\label{subsec:linear_models}

Consider the linear hypothesis class
\[
\H_{\mathrm{lin}} := \{f_\theta : x \mapsto x^\top \theta \;:\; \theta \in \Theta \subseteq \R^p\},
\]
and let $X \in \R^{n\times p}$ denote the feature matrix. For a given coefficient vector $\theta \in \Theta$, we have
$
f_\theta(x_i) = x_i^\top \theta,
$ and $
e := X\theta - y.
$ 
For notation simplicity, let us define
\[
H := I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top,
\qquad
c := Hy = y - \bar y\,\mathbf{1},
\qquad
D := \diag(c_1^2,\dots,c_n^2),
\]
so that
$
\widehat{\Cov}(e,y)
=
\frac{1}{n}e^\top c.
$ 

Throughout this subsection, we take the baseline loss to be
\[
L(f_\theta) = \frac{1}{2n}\|X\theta-y\|_2^2.
\]

\paragraph{Squared covariance penalty in linear models.}
For the symmetric penalty
\[
\Psi^{\mathrm{cov}^2}(f_\theta)
=
\frac{1}{2}\,\widehat{\Cov}_n(e,y)^2
=
\frac{1}{2n^2}\bigl(e^\top c\bigr)^2,
\]
the penalized estimator solves
\begin{align}
    \label{opt:linear_cov2}
    \theta_{\rho}^{\mathrm{cov}^2}
    \in
    \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}
    \left\{
        \frac{1}{2n}\|X\theta-y\|_2^2
        +
        \frac{\rho}{2n^2}\bigl((X\theta-y)^\top c\bigr)^2
    \right\}.
\end{align}
Define
\[
a := X^\top c,
\qquad
b := y^\top c = c^\top c.
\]
Then the first-order optimality condition for \eqref{opt:linear_cov2} is
\[
\left(
\frac{1}{n}X^\top X + \frac{\rho}{n^2}aa^\top
\right)\theta
=
\frac{1}{n}X^\top y + \frac{\rho}{n^2}ba.
\]
Hence, whenever the displayed inverse exists,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:linear_cov2_closed_form}
    \theta_{\rho}^{\mathrm{cov}^2}
    =
    \left(
        \frac{1}{n}X^\top X
        +
        \frac{\rho}{n^2}aa^\top
    \right)^{-1}
    \left(
        \frac{1}{n}X^\top y
        +
        \frac{\rho}{n^2}ba
    \right).
\end{align}
This shows that the squared covariance penalty induces a rank-one modification of the usual normal equations.

\paragraph{Surrogate penalty in linear models.}
For the separable surrogate
\[
\Psi^{\mathrm{surr}}(f_\theta)
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 c_i^2
=
\frac{1}{n}e^\top D\,e,
\]
the penalized problem is
\begin{align}
    \label{opt:linear_surr}
    \theta_{\rho}^{\mathrm{surr}}
    \in
    \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}
    \left\{
        \frac{1}{2n}\|X\theta-y\|_2^2
        +
        \frac{\rho}{n}e^\top D\,e
    \right\}.
\end{align}
The corresponding optimality condition is
\[
\left(
\frac{1}{n}X^\top X
+
\frac{2\rho}{n}X^\top D X
\right)\theta
=
\frac{1}{n}X^\top y
+
\frac{2\rho}{n}X^\top D y,
\]
and therefore, whenever the displayed inverse exists,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:linear_surr_closed_form}
    \theta_{\rho}^{\mathrm{surr}}
    =
    \left(
        \frac{1}{n}X^\top X
        +
        \frac{2\rho}{n}X^\top D X
    \right)^{-1}
    \left(
        \frac{1}{n}X^\top y
        +
        \frac{2\rho}{n}X^\top D y
    \right).
\end{align}

\paragraph{Extension to regularized linear models.}
The same constructions extend immediately to penalized linear estimators of the form
\[
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}
\left\{
    L(f_\theta) + \Omega(\theta) + \rho\,\Psi(f_\theta)
\right\},
\]
if \(\Omega\) is a convex differentiable complexity regularizer on the coefficients. In contrast, in non-differentiable cases such as the LASSO, the problem remains convex but no longer admit closed-form expressions, and must instead be solved numerically. These Linear models solutions show that the method can be integrated into standard regularized regression with minimal conceptual disruption.

\subsubsection{Covariance-Penalized Second-Order Tree Boosting}
\label{subsec:tree_boosting}

We next consider second-order additive tree boosting under squared loss, as used in gradient-boosted tree systems such as XGBoost and LightGBM. Let \(f^{(m-1)}\) denote the current prediction function at boosting iteration \(m\), and write
\[
f_i := f^{(m-1)}(x_i),
\qquad
e_i := f_i - y_i,
\qquad
c_i := y_i - \bar y,
\qquad
z := \widehat{\Cov}_n(e,y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i c_i.
\]
At step \(m\), one adds a regression tree \(t\) to the current predictor, so that
\[
f^{(m)}(x) = f^{(m-1)}(x) + \nu\,t(x),
\]
where \(\nu\in(0,1]\) is the learning rate.

\paragraph{Adding dependence penalties into the boosting objective.}
For standard second-order boosting, the objective is approximated locally by
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_local_objective}
    \widetilde{\mathcal J}(t)
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^n
    \left(
        g_i\,t_i + \frac{1}{2}h_i\,t_i^2
    \right)
    +
    \Omega(t),
\end{align}
where \(t_i := t(x_i)\), \(\Omega(t)\) is the tree-complexity penalty, and
\[
g_i := \frac{\partial}{\partial f_i}
\left(
L(f) + \rho\,\Psi(f)
\right),
\qquad
h_i := \frac{\partial^2}{\partial f_i^2}
\left(
L(f) + \rho\,\Psi(f)
\right)
\]
are evaluated at the current predictions. Under squared loss,
\[
\frac{\partial L(f)}{\partial f_i} = \frac{e_i}{n},
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 L(f)}{\partial f_i^2} = \frac{1}{n}.
\]
The covariance-based penalties modify these quantities as follows.

\paragraph{Squared covariance penalty in boosting objective.}
For \(\Psi^{\mathrm{cov}^2}(f)=\tfrac12 z^2\), the gradient is
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_cov2_gradient}
    g_i^{\mathrm{cov}^2}
    =
    \frac{e_i}{n}
    +
    \frac{\rho}{n}z\,c_i.
\end{align}
The exact Hessian of the penalized objective with respect to the prediction vector \(f=(f_1,\dots,f_n)\) is
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_cov2_exact_hessian}
    \nabla_f^2
    \bigl(L(f)+\rho\,\Psi^{\mathrm{cov}^2}(f)\bigr)
    =
    \frac{1}{n}I
    +
    \frac{\rho}{n^2}cc^\top.
\end{align}
Unlike the separable boosting objectives used in standard libraries, \eqref{eq:boosting_cov2_exact_hessian} is dense: each observation is coupled to every other observation through the rank-one term \(cc^\top\). Consequently, there are two natural implementation routes.

The first is a practical approximation that keeps only the diagonal terms,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_cov2_diag_hessian}
    h_i^{\mathrm{cov}^2,\mathrm{diag}}
    =
    \frac{1}{n}
    +
    \frac{\rho}{n^2}c_i^2.
\end{align}
This produces a custom objective compatible with standard second-order boosting APIs, at the price of ignoring the cross-observation curvature induced by the covariance penalty.

The second is an exact leaf-wise implementation for a fixed tree structure. Suppose a candidate tree \(t\) has leaves \(I_1,\dots,I_J\) and leaf weights \(w=(w_1,\dots,w_J)\), so that \(t_i=w_j\) for \(i\in I_j\). Let
\[
n_j := |I_j|,
\qquad
b_j := \sum_{i\in I_j} e_i,
\qquad
s_j := \sum_{i\in I_j} c_i,
\]
and let \(s=(s_1,\dots,s_J)^\top\). If \(\Omega(t)=\gamma J + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_2^2\), then the exact local objective for the squared covariance penalty can be written as
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_cov2_exact_leaf_objective}
    \widetilde{\mathcal J}_{\mathrm{cov}^2}(w)
    =
    g_T^\top w
    +
    \frac{1}{2}w^\top A_T w
    +
    \gamma J
    + \text{const},
\end{align}
where
\[
g_{T,j}
=
\frac{b_j}{n}
+
\frac{\rho}{n}z\,s_j,
\qquad
A_T
=
\diag\!\left(\frac{n_1}{n}+\lambda,\dots,\frac{n_J}{n}+\lambda\right)
+
\frac{\rho}{n^2}ss^\top.
\]
Hence the optimal leaf weights satisfy
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_cov2_exact_leaf_system}
    A_T w^\star = -g_T,
    \qquad
    w^\star = -A_T^{-1}g_T,
\end{align}
whenever \(A_T\) is invertible. The associated gain for the fixed structure \(T\) is
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_cov2_exact_gain}
    \operatorname{Gain}(T)
    =
    \frac{1}{2}g_T^\top A_T^{-1}g_T - \gamma J.
\end{align}
This exact route preserves the full curvature of the covariance penalty, but requires a custom tree-construction procedure rather than the usual observation-wise Hessian interface.

\paragraph{Surrogate penalty as a sample-additive boosting objective.}
For the surrogate
\[
\Psi^{\mathrm{surr}}(f)
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 c_i^2,
\]
the gradient and Hessian remain observation-wise separable:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:boosting_surr_grad_hess}
    g_i^{\mathrm{surr}}
    &=
    \frac{e_i}{n}
    +
    \frac{2\rho}{n}e_i c_i^2,
    \\
    h_i^{\mathrm{surr}}
    &=
    \frac{1}{n}
    +
    \frac{2\rho}{n}c_i^2.
\end{align}
Accordingly, the surrogate penalty is particularly well suited to off-the-shelf second-order boosting implementations: once the weights \(c_i^2\) are precomputed, the penalized objective can be supplied through standard custom gradient and Hessian routines.

\paragraph{Implementation summary.}
The squared covariance penalty is the most direct implementation of the covariance-control idea, but it introduces dense curvature (Hessian); in practice, it can be handled either through a diagonal Hessian approximation in standard boosting libraries (XGBoost or LightGBM), or through an exact custom leaf solver of the form \eqref{eq:boosting_cov2_exact_leaf_system}. The surrogate penalty is fully sample-additive, and is therefore the most convenient to implement through standard observation-wise gradient and Hessian interfaces.

The implementation details in this section show that the covariance-based framework is compatible with both linear estimators and high-performing nonlinear learners. This model-specific flexibility is important for the practical goal of the paper: regressivity correction can be layered onto standard predictive tools already used in assessment workflows, rather than requiring a separate valuation architecture.

\section{Multi-Objective Model Selection Across Folds}
\label{subsec:model_selection}



 After fitting the implemented model families (with or without covariance-based penalization) the practical task remains to compare the out-of-sample performance of the different model or configurations, and to select a final candidate for deployment; a calibration step.
 
The difference with one-dimensional model calibration is that, once regressivity-aware penalties are introduced, candidate models may trade off predictive performance. That is, different model can achieve different tradeoffs between, regressivity, dispersion, and other ratio-related measures, in different ways. Accordingly, model selection can be naturally formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem.

Let $\mathcal V=\{1,\dots,V\}$ index the validation folds (or rolling-origin validation windows), and let $V_v$ denote the validation sample associated with fold $v\in\mathcal V$. Let $\mathcal C$ denote a finite set of candidate model configurations. Each configuration $c\in\mathcal C$ specifies
(i) a model family from the implemented classes,
(ii) a penalty choice in
$
\Psi \in \{0,\Psi^{\mathrm{cov}^2},\Psi^{\mathrm{surr}}\},
$
where $\Psi=0$ denotes the unpenalized baseline,
and
(iii) all associated tuning parameters, including the penalty level $\rho\ge 0$ when applicable.

For each fold $v\in\mathcal V$, fitting configuration $c$ on the corresponding training portion yields a predictor $f_{c,v}$. For each observation $i\in V_v$, let
$
\hat y_i^{\,v}(c):=f_{c,v}(x_i^{\,v})$, $
\hat P_i^{\,v}(c):=\exp\!\bigl(\hat y_i^{\,v}(c)\bigr),$
\text{ and }
$r_i^{\,v}(c):=\frac{\hat P_i^{\,v}(c)}{P_i^{\,v}},
$
where $P_i^{\,v}$ is the observed sale price. 

From the validation predictions on fold $v$, we compute a predictive performance error score $\mathrm{E}_v(c)$ (e.g., mean-squared-error), together with a collection of fold-level assessment diagnostics $Q_{m,v}(c)$ indexed by $m$ (e.g., PRD, VEI, and COD). 

% If one starts from an error metric $E_v(c)$ that is minimized, such as MSE, RMSE, or MAE, one may simply set $\mathrm{Perf}_v(c):=-E_v(c)$.

To aggregate results across folds, let $\alpha_v\ge 0$ satisfy $\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\alpha_v=1$, and define
\[
\bar{\mathrm{Perf}}(c):=\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\alpha_v\,\mathrm{Perf}_v(c),
\qquad
\bar Q_m(c):=\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\alpha_v\,Q_{m,v}(c).
\]
The default choice is uniform weighting, $\alpha_v=1/V$, although the same formulation also allows non-uniform weights when one wishes to emphasize, for example, more recent validation windows.


\paragraph{Model Selection Formulation.} 
We select a single deployed model by solving
\begin{align}
    \label{opt:model_selection_single}
    c^\star
    \in
    \arg\max_{c\in\mathcal C}
    \quad
    & \bar{\mathrm{Perf}}(c)
    \\
    \text{s.t.}\quad
    & \underline{\kappa}_m \le \bar Q_m(c) \le \overline{\kappa}_m,
    \qquad \forall m\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm{int}},
    \nonumber
    % & \bar Q_m(c) \le \overline{\kappa}_m,
    % \qquad \forall m\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm{up}}.
    % \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\mathcal M$ indexes diagnostics constrained to lie in admissible intervals.

Formulation \eqref{opt:model_selection_single} deliberately avoids collapsing all validation criteria into a one-dimensional weighted score. This way, predictive performance remains as the objective, while ratio-study, regressivity, and dispersion diagnostics enter as explicit feasibility requirements. This mirrors the logic of assessor calibration more closely than a purely accuracy-driven ranking rule: among all candidate specifications that satisfy the chosen assessment-quality requirements, select the one with the best aggregated out-of-sample predictive performance.

The formulation above uses aggregated fold-level diagnostics as the default choice. When temporal stability is especially important, one may replace some aggregated constraints with foldwise requirements of the form
\[
Q_{m,v}(c)\in[\underline{\kappa}_m,\overline{\kappa}_m]
\qquad
\forall v\in\mathcal V, \forall m \in \mathcal M
\]
or, more generally, use a more conservative fold aggregator. We keep the weighted-average formulation as the main presentation because it yields a transparent calibration problem over a finite candidate set and aligns naturally with standard validation practice.


\subsubsection{Alternative Pareto-optimal Criteria}

The constrained perspective presented in \eqref{opt:model_selection_single} is not a perfect model-selection criterion, for many reasons. Some of the mos relevant are: (i) This formulation does not secure the constraints are satisfied in out-of-sample sets, (ii) the constraints may not be feasible in the validation sets aggregation (i.e., no configuration can satisfy all of the constraints), and (iii) The sacrifice in prediction performance to obtain those constraint level metrics can be too large. One may modify the level of constraint defined for each metric. For example, given the constraint intervals defined in Table \ref{tab:assessment_metrics_summary}, we could relax them up to certain point by expanding $[\underline \kappa_m, \overline \kappa_m]$. However this method adds more parametric-dependence to the model selection, which misses the point of the single-model selection process.

As alternative criteria, which does not depend on specific metrics targets/constraint intervals, such as ($[\underline \kappa_m, \overline \kappa_m]$, like the ones defined in Table \ref{tab:assessment_metrics_summary}, we propose two alternative criteria: (i) closest-to-Utopian criterion, and (ii) Nash equilibrium (or maximum-volume criterion); both briefly introduced below and illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:pareto_solutions}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Fairness Price/GE Paper/img/model-selection/extreme_case_3_constraint_O1_0.90_edit.pdf}
    \caption{Example of the characterization of the three proposed Pareto-optimal solutions discussed in a convex Pareto-frontier setting, with two objective metrics. The solutions are: constrained formulation (threshold-dependent), closest to Utopian solution, and the Nash equilibrium solution (or maximum-volume criterion).}
    \label{fig:pareto_solutions}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Closest-to-Utopian.} This solution selects the configuration $c\in \C$ that is the closest to an ideal (Utopian) point, in terms of the different aggregated metrics (predictive performance, regressivity, dispersion, etc.). We define this Utopian point as a reference point of the aggregated metrics, which is constructed by taking the best performance obtained on each metric/dimension. In the $[0,1]$-normalized-metrics example in Figure \ref{fig:pareto_solutions}, this solution is the vector $(1,1)$. Note that, to make the metrics comparable between them, we need to use some normalization for each metrics dimension.


\paragraph{Nash equilibrium (max-volume).} This solution selects the configuration $c\in \C$ that produces aggregated-metrics point that sacrifices the least for all metrics, relative to the rest of them. That is, if searching for a proportionally optimal point (a Nash equilibrium), then this is the best option. Since this is a proportional equilibrium, the scale of the metrics does not matter, but just the sign. In the $[0,1]$-normalized-metrics example in Figure \ref{fig:pareto_solutions}, this solution is the vector $(0.76,0.89)$.



\subsection{Extension: Convex Stacking}
\label{subsubsec:convex_stacking}

A natural extension of \eqref{opt:model_selection_single} is to allow convex combinations of candidate predictions rather than selecting only one configuration. This does not change the objective--constraint logic of the calibration step; it changes only the feasible set. In the single-model case, the decision variable is a discrete configuration \(c\in\mathcal C\), and calibration is therefore a constrained sorting problem over finitely many candidates. In the stacking case, the decision variable becomes a nonnegative weight vector on the simplex, and calibration becomes a constrained optimization problem over those weights.

Let $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{stack}}=\{c_1,\dots,c_M\}\subseteq\mathcal C$ denote the set of candidate configurations admitted into the stacking pool. For each fold $v\in\mathcal V$ and observation $i\in V_v$, let
$
\hat P_{i,j}^{\,v}:=\hat P_i^{\,v}(c_j)
$
denote the out-of-fold predicted assessed value on the original price scale produced by configuration $c_j$. For a weight vector
\[
w=(w_1,\dots,w_M)\in\Delta_M
:=
\left\{
w\in\mathbb R_+^M : \sum_{j=1}^M w_j = 1
\right\},
\]
define the stacked prediction on fold $v$ by
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:stacked_prediction}
    \hat P_i^{\,v}(w)
    :=
    \sum_{j=1}^M w_j\,\hat P_{i,j}^{\,v},
    \qquad
    r_i^{\,v}(w)
    :=
    \frac{\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)}{P_i^{\,v}}.
\end{align}
We emphasize that stacking is defined on the \emph{price scale}, not on the log-prediction scale. This choice is natural because the assessment diagnostics entering the constraints are themselves ratio-study quantities defined in terms of assessed values and sale prices.

Given \eqref{eq:stacked_prediction}, one can compute fold-level performance and assessment diagnostics exactly as in the single-model case, now viewed as functions of the weights:
\[
\mathrm{Perf}_v(w),
\qquad
Q_{m,v}(w).
\]
The stacked calibration problem is then
\begin{align}
    \label{opt:model_selection_stacking}
    w^\star
    \in
    \arg\max_{w\in\Delta_M}
    \quad
    & \sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\alpha_v\,\mathrm{Perf}_v(w)
    \\
    \text{s.t.}\quad
    & \underline{\kappa}_m
      \le
      \sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\alpha_v\,Q_{m,v}(w)
      \le
      \overline{\kappa}_m,
      \qquad
      m\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm{int}},
      \nonumber\\
    & \sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\alpha_v\,Q_{m,v}(w)
      \le
      \overline{\kappa}_m,
      \qquad
      m\in\mathcal M_{\mathrm{up}}.
      \nonumber
\end{align}
Thus, the stacking extension preserves the same fold structure, the same diagnostics, and the same feasibility logic as \eqref{opt:model_selection_single}; it simply replaces discrete candidate comparison by continuous optimization over convex combinations of their out-of-fold predictions.

Once an optimal weight vector $w^\star$ has been identified, the final deployed stacked predictor is obtained by refitting each constituent configuration $c_j\in\mathcal C_{\mathrm{stack}}$ on the full pre-assessment development sample and combining their resulting price-scale predictions using the weights $w^\star$.

\subsubsection{Illustrative convex example}
\label{subsubsec:convex_example}

A useful special case is obtained by taking average validation MSE as the objective and imposing worst-fold box constraints on selected ratio-study diagnostics. Let \(n_v:=|V_v|\). For each fold \(v\), define the stacked MSE
\[
\mathrm{MSE}_v(w)
:=
\frac{1}{n_v}\sum_{i\in V_v}
\bigl(\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)-P_i^{\,v}\bigr)^2.
\]
Consider the problem
\begin{align}
    \label{opt:stacking_convex_example}
    \min_{w\in\Delta_M}
    \quad
    & \frac{1}{V}\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\mathrm{MSE}_v(w)
    \\
    \text{s.t.}\quad
    & \underline{\tau}_{\mathrm{mean}}
      \le
      \frac{1}{n_v}\sum_{i\in V_v}\frac{\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)}{P_i^{\,v}}
      \le
      \overline{\tau}_{\mathrm{mean}},
      \qquad \forall v\in\mathcal V,
      \nonumber\\
    & \underline{\tau}_{\mathrm{wmean}}
      \le
      \frac{\sum_{i\in V_v}\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)}{\sum_{i\in V_v}P_i^{\,v}}
      \le
      \overline{\tau}_{\mathrm{wmean}},
      \qquad \forall v\in\mathcal V,
      \nonumber\\
    & \underline{\tau}_{\mathrm{PRD}}
      \le
      \frac{
        \frac{1}{n_v}\sum_{i\in V_v}\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)/P_i^{\,v}
      }{
        \sum_{i\in V_v}\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)\big/\sum_{i\in V_v}P_i^{\,v}
      }
      \le
      \overline{\tau}_{\mathrm{PRD}},
      \qquad \forall v\in\mathcal V.
      \nonumber
\end{align}
The objective in \eqref{opt:stacking_convex_example} is convex quadratic in \(w\), because each \(\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)\) is affine in \(w\). The mean-ratio and weighted-mean-ratio constraints are linear in \(w\). Moreover, the PRD box constraints can be rearranged into linear inequalities on each fold. Indeed, the lower and upper PRD bounds are equivalent to
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:prd_linearized_bounds}
    \frac{1}{n_v}\sum_{i\in V_v}\frac{\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)}{P_i^{\,v}}
    -
    \underline{\tau}_{\mathrm{PRD}}
    \frac{\sum_{i\in V_v}\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)}{\sum_{i\in V_v}P_i^{\,v}}
    &\ge 0,
    \qquad \forall v\in\mathcal V,
    \\
    \frac{1}{n_v}\sum_{i\in V_v}\frac{\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)}{P_i^{\,v}}
    -
    \overline{\tau}_{\mathrm{PRD}}
    \frac{\sum_{i\in V_v}\hat P_i^{\,v}(w)}{\sum_{i\in V_v}P_i^{\,v}}
    &\le 0,
    \qquad \forall v\in\mathcal V,
    \nonumber
\end{align}
and both sides are affine in \(w\). Consequently, \eqref{opt:stacking_convex_example} is a convex quadratic program over the simplex.

This example illustrates the main appeal of the stacking extension. In the single-model case, calibration sorts a finite set of candidate configurations and selects the best feasible one. In the stacking case, the same assessor-facing diagnostics can be enforced while optimizing continuously over nonnegative combinations of candidate predictors, which can enlarge the attainable accuracy--equity trade-off frontier.

\subsection{Effect of Covariance Penalization}

% We next evaluate whether the proposed covariance-based penalties correct the value-dependent ratio trend induced by the nonlinear baseline. 

The regressive mechanism is visible directly in the ratio--value plots. Under the baseline LightGBM model, the fitted ratio pattern exhibits a strong negative trend, with reported linear slope approximately \(-0.2156\) and ratio--price correlation approximately \(-0.2666\). Under the squared covariance penalty, these values move to approximately \(-0.1263\) and \(-0.1224\), respectively. Under the surrogate penalty, they move further toward zero, to approximately \(-0.0222\) and \(-0.0239\). 

These slope and correlation summaries are not the final assessor-facing evaluation criteria by themselves, but they are useful mechanism diagnostics. They show that the training-time correction acts in the intended direction: as the penalty is introduced, the fitted assessment ratios become less systematically related to property value.

\subsection{Penalized LightGBM Performance on the Test Set}

Table~\ref{tab:penalized_models} reports the main held-out test results for representative penalized LightGBM configurations. The main empirical pattern is clear. Relative to the unconstrained LightGBM baseline, the penalized models move the vertical-equity diagnostics sharply toward assessor-preferred ranges. At the same time, the best squared-covariance configurations remain competitive on accuracy and horizontal equity, and in some cases improve them.

The strongest example in the current results is \texttt{LGBMCovPenalty [v2]}. Relative to baseline LightGBM, it improves \(R^2\) from \(0.860\) to \(0.880\), lowers MAE from \$73{,}398 to \$70{,}855, lowers COD from \(21.647\) to \(21.467\), and lowers COV from \(0.330\) to \(0.317\). This is exactly the type of middle-ground behavior the method is designed to obtain.

\subsection{Accuracy--Equity Tradeoffs}

The penalized models do not simply trace a degenerate path in which better vertical equity is purchased by large losses in predictive fit. Instead, increasing \(\rho\) generates a meaningful frontier. In PRD--\(R^2\) space, the penalized models move away from the unconstrained LightGBM point and toward the assessor compliance region while staying close to the upper edge of the accuracy frontier. A similar pattern appears in VEI--\(R^2\) space.

This is the empirical reason to treat calibration as a constrained model-selection problem rather than as pure accuracy ranking. Once multiple assessment diagnostics matter simultaneously, the practical question is no longer which model is best on average in one metric, but which model delivers the strongest predictive performance among those that satisfy the chosen equity requirements.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Assessor Workflows}

The empirical message is operationally simple. The proposed correction can be inserted into an existing assessor workflow with minimal disruption. It uses the same preprocessing pipeline, the same base model family, and the same downstream diagnostics already familiar to assessors. The only new tuning dimension is the penalty parameter \(\rho\), whose qualitative effect is transparent: larger values tend to reduce value-dependent ratio trends. In that sense, the contribution is not a replacement for current workflows, but a targeted correction layer that can expand the attainable accuracy--equity tradeoff set while remaining easy to calibrate, audit, and communicate.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}

This main goal of this work was to study how to reduce regressivity in ML-based mass appraisal; specially in model settings commonly used by assessor. Secondary objectives included achieving this without requiring a new valuation architecture and with minimal modification of existing pipelines. The main message is that predictive accuracy alone does not guarantee vertical equity: nonlinear learners can achieve strong prediction performance while still producing regressive assessment patterns. To address this, we characterize regressivity as a dependence problem between assessment ratio functionals and price-dependent values; introducing a simple covariance-based regularization layer that can be added to many standard regression objectives. This shifts vertical-equity analysis from a purely ex-post diagnostic exercise toward an in-processing control mechanism during model training (in-processing).

Our results suggest that this approach view is practically useful for assessor workflows. The proposed correction can be incorporated into learners such as linear regression and gradient boosting, preserves the usual training logic of the base models, and adds only one main tuning parameter with a transparent effect on value-related ratio tradeoffs. We show this empirically, using the Cook County Assessor's Office pipeline, with results that improve the equity--accuracy tradeoff and show that a middle-ground solution is feasible: models can remain competitive in predictive performance and horizontal-equity diagnostics while substantially reducing regressivity, and in some settings can move inside the standard compliance ranges.

Another contribution of the paper is methodological. Once equity is treated as an explicit modeling target, model calibration should no longer be viewed as a purely accuracy-based ranking problem. Instead, it is more natural in mass appraisal setting to formulate model calibration as a multi-objective selection problem over predictive performance, dispersion, and vertical-equity diagnostics. In that sense, the paper contributes both a practical correction layer for standard learners and a calibration perspective that is better aligned with the real tradeoffs faced in assessor practice.

\subsection{Limitations}

The proposed framework has several limitations. First, it does not optimize standard assessor-facing vertical-equity diagnostics such as PRD, PRB, or VEI directly; instead, it controls a related dependence pattern that is intended to reduce the regressivity patterns in terms of these measures. Second, we do not currently provide guarantees that regressivity control achieved in training or validation will remain stable out of sample. Third, covariance-based penalties mainly target linear dependence, so more complex nonlinear price-related patterns may remain or even be distorted under aggressive penalization. Fourth, the current penalties are global and are not explicitly aware of sub-market or geographic structure, so they may miss local inequities. Finally, the constrained calibration layer is itself imperfect: feasible constraint satisfaction may fail in some validation windows, and in some cases one may sacrifice too much accuracy in order to satisfy equity thresholds. Finally, the dependence measure itself is not perfect, it must be evaluated in depth by assessors and relevant entities.

\subsection{Future Work}

Several extensions appear especially promising. A first direction is to move beyond linear dependence by studying richer penalties based on nonlinear dependence measures. A second is to incorporate sub-market, neighborhood, or other stratified structure directly into the penalty, so that local inequities are not averaged away by a global correction. A third is to study alternative formulations more closely tied to assessor practice, including price-scale penalization and fairness control layered with other sensitive or policy-relevant attributes. A fourth direction is interpretability: it would be valuable to develop vertically equitable versions of more interpretable learners, or explanation tools for nonlinear penalized models. Finally, future work should test the framework across additional assessor offices and broader model classes, including neural networks, random forests, bagging methods, and model-stacking approaches.

The covariances with $z$ also converge to zero because they are continuous finite-sample functionals of $r_i(f_m)$ and $u_i(f_m)=\log r_i(f_m)$.

% \begin{remark}[Why the monotone assumption is needed]
% \label{rem:cov_not_sufficient}
% A small covariance by itself does not rule out more complex nonmonotone price effects, because positive and negative local trends can cancel in the aggregate. The role of Assumption~\ref{ass:monotone_regressivity} is precisely to align the covariance target with the assessor metrics. Accordingly, the covariance penalty proposed here should not be interpreted as a replacement for PRD, PRB, or VEI, but rather as a smooth training-time control for the first-order directional component of regressivity, whose reduction moves the standard vertical-equity diagnostics in the desired direction under the monotone root behavior described above.

\section{Algorithmic Details for Covariance-Penalized Boosting}
\label{app:boosting_algorithms}

This appendix collects pseudocode for the boosted-tree implementation of the squared covariance penalty introduced in Section~\ref{sec:covariance_penalty_learning}. Throughout, we use the manuscript's sign convention
\[
e_i = f(x_i)-y_i,
\qquad
c_i = y_i-\bar y,
\qquad
z = \widehat{\Cov}_n(e,y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i c_i.
\]
Under this convention, the squared covariance penalty is
$
\Psi^{\mathrm{cov}^2}(f)=\frac{1}{2}z^2.
$

\subsection{Exact Newton-style tree boosting with squared covariance penalty}
\label{app:exact_newton_cov_boosting}

The following algorithm implements the exact leaf-wise Newton update discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:tree_boosting}.

\begin{algorithm}[!ht]
\caption{Exact Newton-style tree boosting with squared covariance penalty}
\label{alg:exact_newton_cov_boosting}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training data $\D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, penalty weight $\rho \ge 0$, leaf regularization $\lambda \ge 0$, tree complexity penalty $\gamma \ge 0$, learning rate $\nu \in (0,1]$, number of rounds $M$
\State Compute $\bar y \gets \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i$
\State Compute $c_i \gets y_i-\bar y$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$
\State Initialize $f_0(x) \gets \bar y$
\For{$m=1,\dots,M$}
    \State Compute current predictions $f_i \gets f_{m-1}(x_i)$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$
    \State Compute residuals $e_i \gets f_i-y_i$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$
    \State Compute covariance statistic
    \[
    z \gets \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i c_i
    \]
    \State Compute exact gradients
    \[
    g_i \gets \frac{e_i}{n} + \frac{\rho}{n} z c_i
    \qquad \forall i=1,\dots,n
    \]
    \State Search over candidate tree structures $T$
    \For{each candidate tree $T$ with leaves $I_1,\dots,I_J$}
        \For{$j=1,\dots,J$}
            \State $G_j \gets \sum_{i\in I_j} g_i$
            \State $n_j \gets |I_j|$
            \State $s_j \gets \sum_{i\in I_j} c_i$
        \EndFor
        \State Form
        \[
        D \gets \diag\!\left(\frac{n_1}{n}+\lambda,\dots,\frac{n_J}{n}+\lambda\right),
        \qquad
        \beta \gets \frac{\rho}{n^2},
        \qquad
        A_T \gets D + \beta ss^\top
        \]
        \State Compute exact tree gain
        \[
        \operatorname{Gain}(T)
        \gets
        \frac{1}{2}G^\top A_T^{-1}G - \gamma J
        \]
    \EndFor
    \State Select the best tree structure $T_m$
    \State For the selected tree, form $A_m \gets D + \beta ss^\top$
    \State Compute optimal leaf weights
    \[
    w^\star \gets -A_m^{-1}G
    \]
    \State Update
    \[
    f_m(\cdot) \gets f_{m-1}(\cdot) + \nu\,T_m(\cdot;w^\star)
    \]
\EndFor
\State \Return final predictor $f_M$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
